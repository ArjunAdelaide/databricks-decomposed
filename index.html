The thesis

Not a warehouse company. A data‑gravity company.

Storage decoupled from compute on open table formats. From that moment, the marginal cost of pointing a new workload at data you already govern approaches zero. Every Databricks business line is a consequence of that one fact.

The business model

Who pays, and for what.

Strip away the product names and Databricks is a consumption business: a software layer metered by the second, sold against data that is expensive to move and cheap to build on.

Who pays

Data teams, out of the cloud budget

The buyer is the head of data platform or the CDO, and the bill comes out of infrastructure spend, not a per-seat software line. Cost scales with how much work runs, not how many people log in. That makes the first dollar easy and the ten-millionth dollar invisible inside a budget that was growing anyway.

The unit

The DBU, burned per second

Everything is priced in Databricks Units: a per-second unit of processing. Customers sign multi-year spend commitments, then draw them down across any product. ETL, SQL, training, and agents all burn the same currency, so a new workload needs a config change, not a new contract.

The margin

Software on rented hardware

Classic compute runs inside the customer's own cloud account; Databricks bills only the software premium on top. Serverless and AI workloads run on infrastructure Databricks rents itself, so cost of revenue rises with GPU prices. That mix shift is the whole story behind gross margin falling from above 80% to about 74%.

The motion

Land small, expand by usage

Land with pipelines and governance, then let consumption grow into the commitment. Renewals price off actual usage, which is how retention stays above 140%. Azure Databricks ships as a first-party Microsoft service, and AWS and GCP marketplace deals burn down cloud commitments the customer already signed, so procurement is mostly pre-cleared.

The flywheel

Land the data once. Sell against it forever.

01 · Open formats

Delta Lake and Apache Iceberg keep data in open files the customer owns. Buying Tabular put the creators of both formats inside one company. No lock-in at the storage layer lowers the cost of saying yes.

02 · Data lands once

Pipelines land raw data into governed object storage, cataloged by Unity Catalog. The switching cost is not the files. It is the permissions, lineage, and audit trail wrapped around them.

03 · Every workload, same copy

ETL, SQL analytics, ML training, and agents all run against the single governed copy. Adding a workload is a configuration change, not a migration project.

04 · Consumption revenue

Every workload burns DBUs, billed per second. More workloads on the same data means more revenue without a new sales cycle. Net revenue retention runs above 140%.

05 · R&D and acquisitions

Consumption cash funds the next engine: MosaicML for $1.3B, Tabular for a reported $1B to $2B, Neon for about $1B, Tecton in stock.

06 · More workloads viable

Each acquisition bolts a new workload onto the same copy of data. The marginal customer gets more reasons to land data, and the loop closes.

01 / Lakehouse

The land‑and‑govern layer.

Delta Lake tables, Lakeflow pipelines, Unity Catalog governance. Priced in DBUs, billed on consumption. It runs on all three clouds, and Azure Databricks ships as a first-party Microsoft service, which puts the product inside Microsoft's own sales motion.

Who pays: data engineering and platform teams. The unit of consumption: compute-seconds against governed data. The margin lives in software orchestrating cloud hardware the customer already rents.

Strategic role: the low-friction landing zone. Platform revenue is not the point. The governed copy of data is the point, because it is what the other two arms monetize.

02 / SQL

The attack on Snowflake.

Databricks SQL points BI tools at the same governed copy of data instead of loading a second copy into a separate warehouse. It reached a $600M run rate by December 2024, the company projected $1B by January 2026, and Sacra estimates roughly $1.5B by mid-2026. The pitch is not a better warehouse. It is the deletion of a duplicate.

03 / AI

The wager on agents.

MosaicML, bought for $1.3B in 2023, became Mosaic AI: training, serving, evaluation. DBRX in 2024 proved the training stack in public. Agent Bricks builds agents on the customer's own governed data. Lakebase, built from the ~$1B Neon acquisition, gives those agents a transactional Postgres memory next to the lakehouse. Tecton added real-time feature serving.

The bet: enterprises will build agents on their own governed data, and the platform that already holds the data wins the training and inference workloads. AI products ran at $1.7B annualized in June 2026, up from $1.4B four months earlier.

The honest caveat: this arm is where the spend concentrates. Gross margin fell from above 80% to about 74% in a year as GPU and cloud infrastructure costs took hundreds of millions in margin dollars. Every hyperscaler sells an agent stack next to its own storage. Data gravity is a real advantage. It is not yet a proven moat against companies that own the data centers.

Synthesis

How the arms compound.

Platform

The landing zone

Sells first, on pipelines and governance. Low friction, small initial consumption. Its job is to make the data arrive and make it governed.

SQL

The expansion engine

Points existing BI budgets at data already landed. No migration, no second copy. This is where net revenue retention above 140% is manufactured.

AI

The option on the future

If agents run on governed enterprise data, the platform holding that data collects the training and inference bill. Expensive to build, priced like an option.

Still private. On purpose.

No S-1 has been filed. In June 2026, CEO Ali Ghodsi called 2026 "a terrible year to go public" and pointed to 2027 at the earliest. The Series L, closed in February 2026 at $7B+ including roughly $2B of debt capacity, included employee tender liquidity, which removes the main internal pressure to list.

Why it matters: private capital is currently willing to fund the AI buildout, margin compression included, at prices public markets have not had to underwrite. Staying private lets Databricks spend through the land-grab phase without quarterly scrutiny of that 80%-to-74% margin slide. For scale: Snowflake's public market cap was about $83B when Databricks priced at $134B.
